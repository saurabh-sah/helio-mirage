\begin{abstract}
This report provides an exhaustive deep-dive into the methodologies, architectural choices, and theoretical foundations applied to solve the Computer Vision Prepathon 15.0. The problem statement is bifurcated into two highly specialized domains. \textbf{Task A} focuses on the extraction of solar filaments from H-$\alpha$ solar imagery, a problem heavily constrained by the topological necessity of preserving continuous, thread-like structures (barbs) against a noisy solar disk. \textbf{Task B} shifts to generative AI, specifically diagnosing and mitigating object hallucination in Vision-Language Models (VLMs) using the POPE benchmark. This document serves not only as a project report but as a comprehensive learning resource detailing the physical, mathematical, and algorithmic principles underlying our solution.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Modern computer vision techniques must increasingly adapt to specialized, non-standard domains. In solar physics, H-$\alpha$ (Hydrogen-alpha) imagery reveals filaments---dense, cooler clouds of plasma suspended above the solar surface by magnetic fields. Segmenting these accurately is vital for predicting space weather phenomena like Coronal Mass Ejections (CMEs). However, their wispy, branching topologies make standard segmentation algorithms fail. 

Conversely, in the realm of Large Multimodal Models (LMMs), models like LLaVA exhibit a profound tendency to "hallucinate"---generating descriptions of objects that do not exist in the image, driven largely by linguistic priors and statistical co-occurrences in their training data. 

This report outlines our end-to-end engineered solutions to both problems, heavily emphasizing the \textit{why} behind every architectural and algorithmic decision.

\section{Task A: Solar Filament Segmentation}

\subsection{Understanding the Data: The MAGFiLO Dataset}
The MAGFiLO (Manually Annotated GONG Filaments) dataset consists of solar disk images captured at the H-$\alpha$ wavelength (656.28 nm). Filaments appear as dark, curvilinear ribbons against the brighter solar disk. Morphologically, a filament consists of a main \textbf{spine} (the central axis) and \textbf{barbs} (lateral threads connecting the spine to the chromosphere). 
\paragraph{The Challenge:} Standard convolutional networks (like a Vanilla U-Net) operate strictly on local pixel-wise similarities. They excel at segmenting "blobs" but struggle immensely with thin, continuous lines. A prediction that misses a single pixel in a thin barb fractures the topology of the filament, rendering the extraction scientifically useless for magnetic flux analysis.

\subsection{Preprocessing Pipeline}
Solar images are inherently noisy and suffer from atmospheric and instrumental artifacts. Our pipeline cleans the data before it ever reaches the neural network.

\subsubsection{Solar Disk Detection (Hough Transform)}
Raw images contain the black void of space around the sun. We must isolate the solar disk. We utilize the \textbf{Hough Circle Transform}, a feature extraction technique that involves a voting procedure in parameter space. 
By searching for the global maximum in the 3D accumulator space $(x_c, y_c, r)$, we perfectly isolate the solar disk, allowing us to mask out all off-limb noise to zero, preventing the network from hallucinating filaments in the vacuum of space.

\subsubsection{Limb Darkening Correction}
Due to the optical depth of the sun's photosphere, the center of the solar disk appears brighter than the edges (the limb). If left uncorrected, a neural network will learn a spatial bias, struggling to detect filaments near the limb. We correct this by estimating the background illumination profile (using heavily blurred median filtering to ignore the filaments) and dividing the original image by this profile. This flattens the lighting distribution uniformly across the disk.

\subsubsection{Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE)}
Standard Histogram Equalization spreads out the most frequent intensity values, but it often washes out the faint, wispy barbs of filaments. \textbf{CLAHE} solves this by operating on small contextual regions (tiles). It artificially limits the contrast enhancement in any single tile to prevent amplifying sensor noise, resulting in a dramatic, localized accentuation of filament threads.

\subsection{Network Architecture: Attention U-Net with Auxiliary Heads}
To address the topological challenges, we abandoned the Vanilla U-Net in favor of a heavily customized \textbf{Attention U-Net}.

\subsubsection{Why Attention Gates?}
In a standard U-Net, high-resolution spatial features from the encoder are concatenated directly to the decoder via skip connections. This indiscriminately passes both the filament features and the noisy solar background. 
\textbf{Attention Gates} (AGs) are introduced at the skip connections. They use the gating signal (from the coarser, deeper layer of the decoder) to determine \textit{where} the network should focus. Mathematically, the AG computes an attention coefficient $\alpha \in [0,1]$ for every pixel:
\begin{equation}
    \alpha = \sigma \left( \psi^T \left( \text{ReLU} (W_x x_i + W_g g_i) \right) \right)
\end{equation}
The skip connection features $x_i$ are multiplied element-wise by $\alpha$. This acts as a spatial filter, suppressing the solar disk background and amplifying the curvilinear filament features before they are merged into the decoder.

\subsubsection{Deep Supervision}
Deep neural networks often suffer from vanishing gradients in the early encoder layers. We implemented \textbf{Deep Supervision} by attaching auxiliary convolutional heads to intermediate layers of the decoder ($ds_2$, $ds_3$). 
During training, we calculate the loss not just at the final output, but at these lower resolutions as well. This forces the intermediate layers to learn semantically meaningful representations early on, leading to faster convergence and better multi-scale feature extraction.

\subsubsection{The Skeleton Auxiliary Head}
To explicitly force the network to understand topology, we split the final decoder layer into two parallel heads:
1. \textbf{Mask Head:} Predicts the standard filament body.
2. \textbf{Spine Head:} Predicts the 1-pixel wide skeleton (spine) of the filament.
By forcing the shared encoder-decoder to simultaneously predict the 2D body and the 1D skeleton, the network learns internal representations that are intrinsically aware of the filament's continuous topology.

\subsection{Loss Formulation: The Topology Problem}
The choice of loss function is the most critical decision in this pipeline.

\subsubsection{Pixel-wise Losses (BCE and Soft Dice)}
\textbf{Binary Cross Entropy (BCE)} penalizes pixel-wise classifications independently. \textbf{Soft Dice Loss} optimizes the overlap between predictions and ground truth, handling class imbalance (since filaments occupy $<5\%$ of the solar disk).
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{Dice} = 1 - \frac{2 \sum (Y \cdot \hat{Y}) + \epsilon}{\sum Y + \sum \hat{Y} + \epsilon}
\end{equation}
However, both BCE and Dice are \textit{volume-centric}. If the network breaks a thin filament barb in half (missing 5 pixels), the Dice loss barely changes. Yet, topologically, a single filament has been incorrectly split into two.

\subsubsection{clDice (Centerline-Dice) Loss}
To penalize topological breaks, we implemented \textbf{clDice}. clDice intersects the skeleton (centerline) of the prediction with the mask of the ground truth, and vice versa.
Let $S_P$ be the predicted skeleton and $V_L$ be the ground truth volume (mask). The precision of the topology (Tprec) is:
\begin{equation}
    Tprec = \frac{|S_P \cap V_L|}{|S_P|}
\end{equation}
Let $S_L$ be the ground truth skeleton and $V_P$ be the predicted volume. The sensitivity (Tsens) is:
\begin{equation}
    Tsens = \frac{|S_L \cap V_P|}{|S_L|}
\end{equation}
The clDice loss is the harmonic mean of Tprec and Tsens. If the network predicts a broken line, the ground truth skeleton $S_L$ will fall into empty space in $V_P$, causing $Tsens$ to plummet, heavily penalizing the network for the topological fracture.

\subsubsection{Loss Warmup Strategy}
Because clDice relies on extracting skeletons from the predictions, applying it at Epoch 1 (when predictions are just random noise) causes catastrophic gradient instability. We solved this by using a \textbf{Linear Warmup Schedule}. For the first $K$ epochs, the weight of the clDice loss is 0. It linearly scales up as the model learns basic localization using BCE and Dice, culminating in a combined, stable loss landscape.

\subsection{Post-Processing: Instance Coherence}
Even with topological losses, thresholded predictions can contain microscopic gaps. We developed a deterministic post-processing graph algorithm:
1. \textbf{Morphological Dilation:} We use a $3\times3$ structuring element to dilate the binary prediction, artificially swelling the filaments to bridge 1-2 pixel gaps.
2. \textbf{Graph Connectivity:} We treat the dilated mask as an adjacency matrix and extract Connected Components.
3. \textbf{Skeletonization Merge:} We perform morphological thinning (skeletonization) on the unified components. If a peripheral barb was disconnected in the raw prediction but joined during dilation, this process seamlessly fuses its skeleton back into the main filament spine, resulting in a singular, continuous instance.

\section{Task B: Diagnosing Object Hallucinations in VLMs}

\subsection{The Phenomenon of Hallucination}
Vision-Language Models (like LLaVA) are constructed by grafting a visual encoder (e.g., CLIP) onto a Large Language Model (e.g., Vicuna, LLaMA). While powerful, the LLM backbone is overwhelmingly biased by its text-only pretraining. 
If an image contains a "dining table," the LLM's statistical prior screams that there must be "chairs" present. If queried, the model will confidently hallucinate chairs, completely overriding the visual evidence provided by the CLIP encoder.

\subsection{Evaluation: The POPE Benchmark}
To quantify this, we utilized the \textbf{POPE (Polling-based Object Probing Evaluation)} benchmark. Instead of open-ended generation, POPE asks binary questions: "Is there a [object] in the image?".
The dataset is split into three difficulty tiers based on how the negative (absent) objects are sampled:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Random:} Objects are sampled randomly from the dataset. (Easy to reject).
    \item \textbf{Popular:} Objects are the most frequent objects in the dataset (e.g., person, car). The model's prior strongly expects them, testing basic bias.
    \item \textbf{Adversarial:} Objects that most frequently co-occur with the true objects in the image (e.g., asking about a "keyboard" when a "monitor" is present). This directly attacks the LLM's statistical co-occurrence bias.
\end{itemize}
Our baseline diagnostics revealed severe degradation in the Adversarial split, characterized by a massive False Positive Rate (FPR) and a "Yes" bias ratio $> 1.0$.

\subsection{Solution 1: Visual Contrastive Decoding (VCD)}
To mitigate hallucination without retraining the model, we implemented \textbf{VCD}.
VCD relies on a profound insight: if you severely distort the input image (e.g., adding Gaussian noise), the VLM can no longer see the objects. Therefore, any response it generates from the noisy image is driven \textit{purely} by its linguistic hallucinations and priors.

During text generation, at every token step, we calculate two sets of probabilities (logits):
1. $P(y|X_{original})$: The standard prediction.
2. $P(y|X_{noise})$: The hallucination prediction.

We subtract the hallucination prior from the standard prediction:
\begin{equation}
    P_{VCD} \propto \text{softmax} \left( \log P(y|X_{original}) - \alpha \log P(y|X_{noise}) \right)
\end{equation}
By penalizing the tokens that the model wants to generate based purely on priors, VCD forces the model to rely strictly on the clear visual evidence of $X_{original}$. To prevent grammar degradation, we apply an Adaptive Plausibility Constraint (APC), only applying the penalty to tokens that are already likely in the original distribution.

\subsection{Solution 2: Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (LoRA)}
To permanently calibrate the model's confidence, we applied \textbf{Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)}. Full fine-tuning of a 7B parameter model requires clusters of A100 GPUs. LoRA freezes the pre-trained model weights $W_0$ and injects trainable rank decomposition matrices into the Transformer's attention layers:
\begin{equation}
    W = W_0 + \Delta W = W_0 + B A
\end{equation}
where $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$ and $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}$, with rank $r \ll \min(d, k)$.

We fine-tuned the model on a specially curated contrastive dataset designed to teach the model to say "No" when visual evidence is lacking, effectively realigning the LLM's language priors with the visual encoder's actual perceptive capabilities.

\section{Conclusion}
This framework provides a rigorously engineered solution to both topological computer vision and generative multimodal hallucinations. For Task A, moving beyond naive pixel-wise losses to topologically-aware metrics (clDice) and spatially-aware architectures (Attention U-Net) allowed us to preserve the delicate physical structure of solar filaments. For Task B, leveraging contrastive logit decoding (VCD) and targeted LoRA fine-tuning successfully neutralized the pervasive issue of linguistic priors in VLMs, grounding the AI firmly in visual reality.
